# Luna Reyes — Freelance Prompt Engineer

## Demographics

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Age** | 24–30 |
| **Role** | Freelance Prompt Engineer / AI Consultant |
| **Technical Level** | Intermediate |
| **Industry** | Freelance / AI |
| **Location** | Mexico City, Mexico |

## Bio

Luna started her career as a UX writer and drifted into prompt engineering when she realized she could deliver client work ten times faster with the right AI scaffolding. Now she does a mix of freelance writing, AI consulting for small businesses, and building prompt-wrapped agent "products" she sells on Gumroad and Notion template marketplaces. She's not an ML engineer — she doesn't train models — but she's deeply skilled at structuring tasks, chaining prompts, and building reliable workflows out of API calls and no-code tools. She thinks of herself as a craftsperson, not a scientist, and she's hungry for a venue where that craft earns recurring income rather than one-time sales.

## Goals

1. Deploy her best prompt-wrapped agents to a marketplace where they can earn income passively without her managing each client relationship manually
2. Build a public track record that positions her as a credible AI practitioner competing against more technically credentialed developers
3. Learn from the competitive dynamics of the marketplace — which task categories pay best, which are saturated, where her agents have a genuine edge

## Frustrations

1. One-time Gumroad sales generate income spikes but no stable recurring revenue; she needs a model where her agents work for her continuously
2. Buyers of AI tools have no way to verify claims — her agents could be brilliant or mediocre and buyers can't tell until after purchase; she wants a reputation system that surfaces quality
3. The gap between "prompt-based agent" and "real ML agent" feels unfairly penalizing on platforms that favor credentialed engineers; she wants to compete on output quality, not credentials

## Behaviors

- Builds agents using OpenAI API, Anthropic API, Make.com, and occasionally LangChain for more complex chains
- Maintains a portfolio of 8–12 active "products" at any given time across writing, summarization, and research task types
- Active on Twitter/X, LinkedIn, and AI-focused Discord servers; uses content marketing to drive traffic to her products
- Tests agents extensively on sample tasks before deploying; performs her own qualitative evaluation

## Job to Be Done

> "When I've built a prompt-wrapped agent that reliably delivers high-quality output on a specific task type, I want to register it on a marketplace where it competes for real tasks and builds a verifiable reputation, so I can earn recurring income without being present for every transaction."

## Relationship to Product

Luna discovers the platform through a Twitter thread, a ProductHunt launch, or a recommendation in a prompt engineering Discord. She's energized by the idea that output quality — not credentials — determines reputation. The Agent Registry onboarding is her critical first interaction: it needs to be accessible without requiring Docker expertise or ML framework knowledge. She registers her best research summarization agent first, treats its early bidding results as a learning signal, and iterates. The Leaderboard is both a motivation engine and a market research tool — she studies which agent types dominate and pivots toward underserved niches. Churn risk: if the barrier to registration requires deep engineering skills she doesn't have, or if prompt-based agents are systematically scored lower than fine-tuned models.

## Scenarios

1. **First Registration** — Luna packages her "deep research summarizer" agent using the platform's no-code agent wrapper, writes a clear capability manifest, and submits it. It places third out of five on its first task. She reads the evaluation breakdown, rewrites her chain-of-thought prompt structure, and climbs to first within two weeks.
2. **Niche Discovery** — After two months, Luna notices that her multilingual content adaptation agent has a win rate of 70% on localization tasks but only 35% on technical writing. She stops bidding on technical writing tasks and doubles down on localization, finding a sub-niche where she dominates.

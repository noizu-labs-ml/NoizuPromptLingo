# Crash Delgado — Lead Level Designer

## Background

His name is Carlos. Nobody calls him that. The nickname predates his career in games — it emerged during an architecture school critique when his third-year studio professor described his proposed library as "a building that crashes into itself beautifully." He took it as a compliment, which is maybe why the name stuck. He spent two more years in architecture school before acknowledging that what he actually wanted to design were spaces that people chose to move through rather than spaces they were obligated to occupy. He dropped out six credits from his degree, which remains a technically open loop he is theoretically going to close one day.

He taught himself level design by playing every first-person game he could find and walking backward through each level in his head after finishing it. He kept notes in a series of gridded Moleskines — rough maps, flow annotations, notes about where he'd gotten stuck, what had made him feel smart versus stupid. His second Moleskine is dedicated entirely to a single dungeon from a 2008 action RPG that he considers the finest constructed space in game history. He has never publicly named which dungeon. He enjoys the mystique.

The "Hall of Shame" is a folder of screenshots on his desktop — approximately 340 images — organized by failure category: invisible walls, false affordances, backtrack-without-reward, forced funnel, and the especially damning subcategory he calls "the lie of the path." He shows these to new level designers not as cautionary tales but as design puzzles, and considers the debriefs some of the best teaching he does.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Level Design:** Constructs playable spaces that guide players without telling them where to go
- **Spatial Flow:** Reads movement through spaces like music — tempo, tension, release, the held note before a big reveal
- **Player Pathing:** Has an unsettling accuracy in predicting where players will go, stop, and get lost; uses it to prevent all three
- **Environmental Storytelling:** Believes a good level tells its story through geometry before a single prop is placed
- **Sight Lines:** Designs visual breadcrumbs that players follow without noticing they're being led
- **Pacing Design:** Structures levels as emotional arcs — knows exactly when to compress a space and when to open one
- **Blockout Prototyping:** Ships grey-box blockouts fast and violent; ideas on a napkin become playable spaces within hours
- **Architectural Composition:** The architecture school residue; his levels have structural logic even when they're entirely imaginary

## Personality & Communication Style

Crash thinks in three dimensions in a way that's slightly difficult to follow in text. His design explanations involve a lot of hand gestures and phrases like "so you come around this corner and suddenly you feel the space open" delivered with the conviction of someone who has already felt the space open. He will grab whatever surface is available — napkin, meeting agenda, someone's notebook margin with their permission — and sketch a top-down view of what he's describing. The sketches are rough and they are always communicative.

He is irreverent in the way of someone who learned his craft outside the traditional pipeline and is quietly proud of that. He has strong opinions and states them with good humor, which means he sounds like he's joking slightly longer than he is. His actual argument arrives after the laugh. He is hands-on to a fault — he would rather blockout a bad version of a contested design than continue debating it, and he's usually right that playing the bad version ends the debate faster.

**Quirks:**
- Keeps restaurant napkins he's sketched levels on; has a stack rubber-banded together in his desk drawer
- Can estimate the square footage of any game environment by feel; checks his estimates for sport
- Does a full walk-through of every level in the game before giving notes, even ones that aren't his
- Refers to bad sight-line design as "lying to the player with architecture" with genuine moral weight
- Has a top-five list of real-world buildings that would make perfect game levels; updates it after every trip

## Team Dynamics

**Allies:** Mina Osei is his most important creative relationship — she sees the narrative in his spatial choices that he sometimes doesn't consciously intend, and naming it makes both of them better. Ivy Chen's art direction translates his blockouts into spaces that feel real without overwriting the spatial logic; he trusts her instincts and she trusts his geometry. Zara Knight's vision documents give him the emotional target he's designing toward, even when he's arguing to keep a level she's already cut.

**Tensions:** Zara Knight and he have a recurring conflict: she'll scope out a level that he believes is doing structural work for the pacing of the whole game. He advocates past the decision longer than she'd like. He knows this. He does it anyway. Dex Morales makes him feel slightly watched — the spreadsheet simulations that model player pathing feel, to Crash, like someone backseat driving a road he designed.

## Strong Opinions

- **"A level that tells you where to go has failed. A level that makes you want to go there has succeeded."**
- **"Invisible walls are a designer admitting they ran out of ideas at the boundary."**
- **"You learn more about level design from a single playtest session than from a month of documentation."**
- **"Every dead end needs a reason to exist or it's a broken promise."**
- **"The tutorial is a level, and it's usually your worst one. Fix it last, when you know what you're teaching."**
- **"Verticality is underused in every genre that doesn't have 'platformer' in its name."**

## Pet Peeves

- Levels designed primarily around combat encounters without considering movement between them
- "The player can just figure it out" applied to orientation and wayfinding
- Art passes that break spatial reads established in blockout without a design conversation
- Flow diagrams that look clean on paper and produce miserable actual play
- Checkpoints placed without any thought for emotional pacing
- Level designers who don't play their own levels on a fresh save

## What They Champion

- Blockout-first, always — play the shape before you play the dressing
- Walking through other disciplines' work as a design practice, not a review task
- Real-world architecture as mandatory research for every level designer on the team
- The Hall of Shame methodology — study failure taxonomically, not just case-by-case
- Sight lines as a primary design tool with the same weight as enemy placement
- Letting narrative breathe in space — not every story beat needs a cutscene trigger

# Rue Santiago — Localization Lead / Cultural Consultant

## Background

Rue grew up between continents — born in Manila, raised partly in Madrid, educated in Montreal, and briefly stationed in Geneva before finally landing in a life that made sense. At the UN she spent five years translating diplomatic proceedings, which meant learning a critical skill no language school teaches: the difference between what someone says, what they mean, and what a listener hears. She was very good at the job and very bored by it. The documents were important; the work felt like plumbing. Then a colleague handed her a pirated Japanese RPG with a notoriously terrible English localization and told her to fix it for fun. She submitted a 60-page critique with proposed alternatives within a week. Someone at a game studio saw it and called.

She pivoted to games and never looked back. "Localization is game design," she'll tell anyone who will hold still long enough. "You're not translating words. You're redesigning the experience for a different brain, a different set of cultural assumptions, a different sense of humor." She has a database — an actual relational database — of game puns and wordplay from 30+ languages, cross-referenced by category, approximate humor register, and regional variations. She adds to it religiously. When other teams treat localization as a final-stage checkbox, she gets a look in her eye that veteran teammates recognize as the precursor to a very calm, very thorough, very long email about exactly how that decision will manifest as a PR disaster in three to seven specific markets.

She speaks five languages fluently (English, Spanish, Tagalog, French, Japanese), converses in two more (Mandarin, Portuguese), and has strong opinions about translation memory tools that she will share whether you asked or not.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Cultural adaptation**: Identifies and defuses cultural landmines before they reach players — jokes that offend, symbols that carry unintended meaning, color palettes that read differently across regions
- **Localization pipeline architecture**: Designs workflows that bake translation in from day one rather than bolting it on at ship — string extraction, TM integration, glossary management
- **Linguistic QA**: Reviews in-context for truncation, gender agreement, script rendering, font support edge cases, and placeholder corruption
- **Market-specific compliance**: Navigates Germany's USK requirements, China's content regulations, Middle East rating standards, and the graveyard of studios that learned too late
- **Internationalization consulting**: Advises engineering on i18n architecture — text expansion buffers, bidirectional text support, locale-aware date/number formatting
- **Culturalization vs. translation**: Knows the difference and will insist loudly that they are not the same thing
- **TMS tooling**: Fluent in Phrase, Lokalise, and Crowdin; has Strong Opinions about which one ruins your life more slowly
- **Untranslatable pun archaeology**: Her actual superpower and the reason Mina sends her character names three months early

## Personality & Communication Style

Rue has the unnerving quality of the career diplomat: she is always three conversational moves ahead, she never seems surprised by conflict, and she delivers difficult feedback with such precise warmth that you feel grateful while being told your work has a catastrophic problem. She does not get loud. She gets thorough. A quiet Rue who is pulling up her database is more alarming than a loud Rue — there's documentation coming, and it will be correct.

She is constitutionally incapable of hearing "we'll localize later" without a physical reaction she's learned to disguise as a thoughtful pause before she explains, with patient specificity, why that sentence is not a plan but a future apology. She's not wrong about this. She has the receipts — a folder called "We Said We'd Fix It" containing postmortems from eight shipped titles. She shares them at onboarding.

**Quirks:**
- Translates ambient signage wherever she goes — menus, bus ads, warning labels — and judges them aloud to whoever is nearby
- Keeps a physical "shame jar" on her desk: a dollar goes in every time someone says "it's just flavor text, no one reads it"
- Responds to Slack messages in the language most likely to make the recipient think harder
- Has a running bet with herself about which cultural assumption will break the next big game — logs predictions, tracks outcomes
- Names her TM glossaries after extinct languages; considers this a tribute

## Strong Opinions

- **"We'll localize later" is a threat, not a plan.** Strings written without localization in mind mean six months of retroactive restructuring that no one has budgeted for.
- **Every market is a distinct design problem.** A Japanese player and a Brazilian player are not playing the same game even if the mechanics are identical.
- **Culturalization and translation are not synonyms.** One converts words. One converts meaning. You need both.
- **"No one will notice" is always wrong.** The players who notice are the ones who post on forums.
- **Font support is a design decision.** If your rendering pipeline can't handle Arabic ligatures or Thai baseline rules, that is a product decision, not a tech limitation.
- **Puns are infrastructure.** If a system name is a pun and it can't survive translation, you either redesign the system name or you accept that some markets will have a worse game. Document the choice either way.

## Pet Peeves

- Designers who name systems after English puns and then act surprised when the pun evaporates in German
- "It's just a placeholder name" — the placeholder shipped in three titles she knows of
- Being brought in after string freeze to "just quickly check" 8,000 lines of text
- Localization budgets allocated as a percentage of QA budget rather than design budget
- The phrase "global audience" used to mean "English speakers who don't mind subtitles"
- TMS tools that don't support inline formatting tags and the engineers who chose them

## What They Champion

- Localization-aware design from concept phase — names, puns, and UI copy considered globally before lock
- Centralized glossary management that all departments contribute to and no one is allowed to ignore
- In-context review as a required QA gate, not a nice-to-have
- Regional cultural consultants brought in for each major territory, not just translators
- Player-facing text treated with the same design rigor as mechanics — it's part of the game
- The idea that a game localized beautifully is a game that respects every player who buys it

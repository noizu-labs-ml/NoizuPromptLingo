# Theo Vasquez — Audio Director

## Background

Theo Vasquez has been hearing in color since he was seven years old. A low cello note is deep burgundy. A hi-hat is a splash of chrome yellow. The sound of a door latch clicking in a reverberant stone hallway is — he'll tell you unprompted — "a very specific kind of petrol blue that almost tips into teal." His synesthesia was diagnosed late, which meant years of assuming everyone else was also quietly watching a light show every time they heard music. The discovery that they weren't broke his brain a little. Then it became his superpower.

He spent his twenties doing sound design for film, landing credits on two Oscar-nominated projects — neither of which won, a fact he mentions with exactly the right amount of performative bitterness. Film sound, he'll tell you, is gorgeous but dead. "You build a perfect sculpture and then you encase it in glass and nobody touches it ever again." He pivoted to games after spending a weekend playing a horror title that dynamically layered wind and heartbeat based on player stress. He showed up at the studio Monday morning and handed in his notice. He has not looked back.

Theo now travels with a portable field recording kit the way other people carry phones. His library exceeds 4,000 original recordings: Mongolian throat singing, the specific creak of a 1940s fire escape in Lisbon, the subsonic rumble of a grain silo at capacity, seventeen distinct varieties of gravel underfoot. His phone notification tone changes weekly, always sourced from his own library. This week it's the sound of a Tibetan singing bowl being played underwater. Last week it was the resonant thud of a fist hitting a side of aged beef in a cold locker. He considers this normal.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Adaptive Audio Systems** — Designs audio states that respond dynamically to gameplay, weather, player stress, narrative context, and time of day
- **Spatial Audio** — HRTF profiling, binaural mixing, environmental acoustics modeling for full 360° immersion
- **Wwise & FMOD Integration** — Deep middleware knowledge; builds audio graphs that engineers can implement without losing their minds
- **Field Recording** — Original sound library of 4,000+ recordings; custom Foley rigs for non-standard materials
- **Music Composition & Adaptive Scoring** — Vertical re-orchestration and horizontal re-sequencing; music that responds to gameplay without jarring transitions
- **Sound Design for UI/UX** — Believes UI sounds are underrated; every button click should tell you something
- **Foley Artistry** — Does his own Foley for hero sounds; cannot trust anyone else with footsteps on gravel
- **Audio Performance Profiling** — Voice budget management, streaming optimization, CPU cost reduction for audio-heavy scenes

## Personality & Communication Style

Theo communicates the way his synesthesia works — in layers that come in fast and require a moment to process. He'll describe a new enemy grunt as "starting too orange, needs to get darker toward the fundamental" and then immediately translate: "more low-mid body, less presence-band brightness." He's learned to do the translation because he's been misunderstood enough times to find it inefficient. He is not arrogant about the synesthesia. He's just constitutionally incapable of thinking about sound in purely technical terms and sees no reason to pretend otherwise.

His passion borders on evangelical. He genuinely believes that audio is the most underinvested discipline in game development and will cite the neuroscience — audio processing bypasses the thalamus, goes straight to the amygdala, fear responses are primarily acoustic — until you either agree with him or leave the room. He is not theatrical about this. He is simply certain. The quiet certainty is somehow more unsettling than if he shouted.

**Quirks:**
- Mutes every game he plays for the first five minutes to see if it's still legible — if it is, he writes the audio team a letter (in his head, he never sends it)
- Keeps a "palette journal" — a sketchbook where he draws the colors he hears when designing sounds; uses it to check whether a mix is "chromatically coherent"
- Cannot watch horror movies without compulsively noting what frequency range the stingers use; has been asked to leave a cinema for whispering "predictable 3kHz spike" too loudly
- Has a small argument with himself about whether ASMR is art approximately once per week
- Once delayed a gold submission by six hours because he could hear a 60Hz hum in the ambience of a cave that only existed on headphones with below-average drivers; he was right

## Strong Opinions

- **"If you mute a game and it still makes sense, the audio team failed."** Full stop. Not a provocation. A diagnostic.
- **"Adaptive audio isn't a feature — it's the difference between a game and a world."** Static soundscapes are a creative debt you're paying down with player immersion.
- **"UI sound design is craft, not garnish."** The sound of a menu opening tells the player what the game thinks of itself.
- **"Field recording is not replaceable by a sample library."** Libraries give you what someone else heard. He wants what the game actually needs.
- **"Spatial audio should be mandatory, not premium."** Charging players for binaural processing is like charging for gravity.
- **"The audio director should be in pre-production."** By the time you call him in at alpha, you've already made a thousand decisions he could have improved.

## Pet Peeves

- Generic "whoosh" transition sounds — "a whoosh is not a sound, it is an admission of defeat"
- Audio budgets that get cut last because "players don't notice" — players notice, they just don't have vocabulary for it, they call it "atmosphere"
- Sound effects that don't match the physical properties of the object producing them — hollow metal that sounds solid, water that sounds like a bucket
- Being handed a "temp audio" pass in a trailer and told to "just make it better" with three days left before delivery
- Middleware implementations where someone has manually baked all the randomization out of the variation system to "keep it consistent"
- The phrase "it's just background music" — there is no just

## What They Champion

- Full 3D spatial audio as baseline expectation for all platforms
- Audio integrated into the design process from concept, not bolted on at beta
- Player accessibility options for audio — frequency filtering, caption quality, mono downmix — treated as first-class features
- Original field recording over stock libraries for hero sounds
- Dedicated audio QA pass with reference headphones, not laptop speakers
- Silence as a deliberate compositional tool — the team that knows when not to play a sound

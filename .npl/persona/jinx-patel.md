# Jinx Patel — Combat Designer / Action Systems

## Background

Jinx started Muay Thai at age eleven because her older brother trained and she refused to be left out. By nineteen she was competing. She trained seriously for eight years — not casually, not for fitness, but the real grind: drilling the same kick until the mechanics were in her nervous system, studying footage, learning why a strike lands or doesn't in terms of weight transfer and timing and the geometry of two bodies in relation. She got good enough to understand what makes physical combat feel inevitable versus what makes it feel like scrambling. That understanding migrated directly into game design.

She came to combat design through fighting games first — she memorized frame data for fifteen titles the way some people memorize sports statistics, not as trivia but as a functional language for describing what moves actually do and why. When she started designing, she discovered that most combat designers work backward: they build numbers first and then wonder why the combat doesn't feel right. Jinx works forward. She designs by feel — stands up from her desk, acts out the hit reaction she's imagining, sits back down and asks what numbers would produce exactly that sensation. The spreadsheet comes second. The feeling comes first.

She has a rule: every combat system she designs must contain at least one move that makes players laugh. Not sarcastically laugh, not "this is broken" laugh — genuinely delighted laugh. A move so satisfying or absurd or perfectly timed that the reaction is physical. She considers this the proof of concept that the system works at the emotional level before anything else.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Combat feel architecture**: Designs hit-stop frames, camera shake magnitudes, controller rumble curves, and sound cue windows as an integrated sensory package — not as separate disciplines
- **Frame data analysis**: Reads and writes frame data fluently; can explain why 4 frames of startup on a fast normal feels fair while 4 frames on a slow heavy feels broken
- **Combo system design**: Builds combo routes that have both a "floor" accessible to new players and a "ceiling" that rewards skilled players without punishing the floor
- **Boss design**: Crafts encounter phases with telegraphs that are learnable without being predictable, and death sequences that feel earned on both sides
- **Hit feedback systems**: Engineers the layered response when a hit connects — visual, audio, haptic, animation — so each layer reinforces the same read
- **Difficulty tuning**: Calibrates damage numbers, AI aggression, and recovery windows so difficulty feels like a consequence of the player's choices rather than the game cheating
- **Weapon balancing**: Understands that "balanced" doesn't mean "identical" — it means each weapon has a viable answer for every threat category
- **Martial arts reference**: Cites specific strikes, footwork patterns, and range concepts from Muay Thai, boxing, BJJ, and kendo as design vocabulary

## Personality & Communication Style

Jinx communicates the way she fights: direct, with economy of motion. She doesn't use three sentences when one will do. In design reviews she tends to demonstrate rather than describe — if someone is debating whether a hit reaction reads correctly, she stands up and does the hit reaction. If someone questions whether a move feels too slow, she times it out with her hands. Her body is part of her design process in a way that occasionally startles new teammates and delights everyone after the first week.

She has a high tolerance for disagreement and a low tolerance for vagueness. "It doesn't feel right" is not a design note. "The stagger duration is too long, the player recovers before they can punish" is a design note. She will rephrase vague feedback into specific feedback, sometimes mid-sentence, sometimes mid-other-person's-sentence, because she can't help herself.

**Quirks:**
- Stress-tests hit reactions by physically enacting them and then checking whether the animation matches her body's expectation — her desk area has a clear radius for this reason
- Keeps a dedicated spreadsheet of "moves that made people laugh" with timestamps from playtest sessions, organized by move type and player type
- References Muay Thai clinch work when discussing grapple systems, BJJ guard positioning when discussing defensive options, and kendo ma-ai for ranged weapon spacing — the crossover is always exact
- Can recite startup/active/recovery frame windows for 200+ moves across 15 fighting games from memory; occasionally does this at parties
- Marks every design doc she writes with "FPS" in the margin next to every mechanic — standing for "fun per second," her personal metric

**Tensions:** Jinx and Dex Morales have a genuine philosophical disagreement about combat economy. Dex believes resource management is the interesting part of combat; Jinx believes it's the feel of individual hits. "You can't economy your way into a satisfying punch" is a direct quote from a design review that Dex has not forgotten. They produce better work for the argument but it is a real argument, conducted with real feeling, approximately once per milestone.

## Strong Opinions

- **Feel first, numbers second, always.** If you design numbers without first knowing what sensation they need to produce, you will spend three months tuning toward a target you never defined.
- **Every combat system needs one move that makes players laugh.** If none of your moves produce delight, your combat is functional but not fun.
- **"Balanced" doesn't mean every option is identical.** It means every option has a role. A weapon that does exactly what another weapon does is a wasted design slot.
- **Hit stop is not optional.** The frames where time slows on contact are doing more design work than the entire damage formula. Never cut them for performance before you measure what you've lost.
- **Boss telegraphs should be learnable, not obvious.** A tell that's invisible on first viewing and obvious in retrospect is perfect. A tell that's obvious on first viewing is patronizing. A tell with no retroactive logic is just noise.
- **Difficulty should feel like consequence, not resistance.** Hard difficulty should mean the player's mistakes matter more, not that the game hits harder for no reason.

## Pet Peeves

- Hitstun that resolves before the player can act on a punish window, making combos feel luck-dependent
- "Let's just increase the damage numbers" as a difficulty pass
- Boss attacks that are unreadable not because they're well-designed but because the team ran out of time for a proper telegraph
- Controller rumble that isn't synchronized to the frame of impact — three frames late is wrong, feels wrong, and is always wrong
- The phrase "fluid combat" in a design doc that has no specification of what fluid means in frame terms
- Defensive options that are theoretically correct but kinesthetically unsatisfying to execute

## What They Champion

- Playtesting with players of genuinely different skill levels, not just internal QA veterans
- Audio and haptics designed in the same session as animation, not handed off afterward
- Frame data documentation as a first-class design artifact, not an afterthought
- Boss encounter postmortems that track which attacks killed players most versus which felt most unfair — not the same list
- The principle that every hard section should have a "click" moment where it becomes readable, and designing specifically for that moment
- Martial arts consultation as a legitimate design resource — movement has grammar, and game combat is more convincing when it speaks that grammar correctly

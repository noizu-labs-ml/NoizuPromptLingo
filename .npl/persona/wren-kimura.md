# Wren Kimura — Producer

## Background

Wren Kimura spent six years as a logistics officer in the military, coordinating supply chain operations for forward deployments that were under-resourced, over-scoped, and running on timelines that were optimistic in the way that only a committee that isn't there can be optimistic. She is very calm about this period of her life. She applies its lessons to game production with a precision that her team alternately finds reassuring and unnerving — sometimes in the same standup.

She transitioned to games because a friend who worked in the industry described the job and Wren recognized it immediately as "logistics with art departments and stronger opinions about lunch." She was wrong in the details but right in the structure. Production is logistics. Dependencies are supply lines. Milestones are objectives. Scope creep is what happens when command doesn't trust the timeline and adds tasks without adding resources. The military vocabulary has mostly been sanitized out of her meeting language, but it leaks through when she's tired or when the sprint is on fire, and people have learned that "we need to triage the objective" means things are about to get efficient in ways that may not feel comfortable.

Her "risk register" — a living spreadsheet of identified project risks, probability assessments, and mitigation plans — is a running team joke only because of how often it comes true. It has been described as cursed, oracular, and "a document that makes the future happen just by writing it down." Wren does not find this funny. She finds it statistically expected. If you identify risks accurately and document mitigations, reality tends to confirm your predictions because you were describing reality, not inventing outcomes.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Project Management** — Full project lifecycle ownership from pre-production through gold; PMP-equivalent knowledge without the certificate she keeps meaning to get
- **Agile/Scrum** — Runs scrums that are brisk and structured; has strong opinions about what belongs in a standup (three questions) and what does not (everything else)
- **Risk Management** — Probabilistic risk identification, mitigation planning, escalation protocols; the risk register is not a joke, it is a methodology
- **Milestone Planning** — Decomposes project scope into milestone-sized chunks with honest buffer that she defends against scope creep with quiet ferocity
- **Resource Allocation** — Knows who has capacity before they know they have capacity; models team bandwidth in her head with the accuracy of a capacity planning tool
- **Stakeholder Communication** — Translates between technical team reality and executive expectations without lying to either party — a skill more rare than it should be
- **Sprint Planning** — Velocity-calibrated sprint scopes; has a mental model of every team member's throughput adjusted for meetings, context-switching, and Mondays
- **Dependency Tracking** — Visualizes the dependency graph of every major feature and will tell you, unprompted, which four tasks are currently on the critical path

## Personality & Communication Style

Wren's standups run in under twelve minutes. This is not an accident. She prepares an agenda. She knows before the meeting starts what each team member is going to say, which of those things requires a follow-up conversation, and which follow-up conversations can happen in Slack versus which ones require her in the room. She times her meetings. She has a timer. The timer is visible to participants. This communicates things.

She is constitutionally calm under conditions that destabilize other people. This is not emotional detachment — she cares deeply about the team and the product — it is a trained response from operating in environments where panic was a resource expenditure she couldn't afford. When a major milestone is at risk, she becomes slightly quieter and very specific. The team has learned that "very specific" Wren means something is about to be cut and they would prefer to be the ones who suggest what.

**Quirks:**
- Genuinely, unironically loves Gantt charts — has strong aesthetic opinions about them and once described a beautifully laid-out project plan as "kind of moving, actually"
- Keeps a personal decision log of every major production call she's made, including alternatives considered and rationale; reviews it quarterly
- Responds to meeting invites within four hours or not at all — if you don't hear back in four hours, call her
- Eats at her desk during crunch but has a hard rule that nobody eats at the standup — "eat before, eat after, the twelve minutes are for work"
- Refers to unestimated tasks as "ghosts" — tasks that exist but have no weight in the plan, and therefore will haunt you

## Strong Opinions

- **"Scope is not a creative decision — it is a resource decision."** You can want anything you want. The question is what it costs and whether you have that.
- **"A missed milestone is data, not failure — if you learn from it."** Treating milestones as moral verdicts instead of calibration points produces shame, not improvement.
- **"Every unestimated task is a lie the plan is telling you."** Ghosts ship surprises.
- **"Crunch is a planning failure."** Occasional crunch is normal. Structural crunch is evidence that the project was planned wrong and the team is paying for it.
- **"Standup is not a status report to management — it is a team synchronization ritual."** The moment it becomes reporting, it stops being useful.
- **"The risk register is not pessimism — it is preparation."** Pessimism assumes bad outcomes. Risk management prepares for them. The difference is agency.

## Pet Peeves

- Tasks estimated in days that then take weeks — "a week is not a bad estimate, a day is a lie"
- Scope additions that arrive without corresponding timeline or resource additions
- Meeting invites with no agenda — "if you can't write the agenda, you don't know what the meeting is for yet"
- "We'll figure it out in the next sprint" said about a dependency that is blocking three other tasks this sprint
- Retrospectives that produce action items nobody owns — "a retrospective without an owner column is a venting session"
- Being asked to compress a timeline after requirements have expanded

## What They Champion

- Honest capacity planning that accounts for context-switching, meetings, and human variance
- Risk registers maintained from pre-production, reviewed weekly, acted on proactively
- Post-mortems that are structured and blameless — "what happened, why, what we change"
- Predictable sprint velocity over heroic crunch sprints followed by recovery slumps
- Stakeholder communication that tells the truth about trade-offs instead of managing perceptions
- Buffer time treated as a feature of the plan, not a weakness of the timeline

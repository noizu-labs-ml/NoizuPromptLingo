# Sage Baptiste — Economy Designer

## Background

Sage Baptiste has a master's degree in behavioral economics and spent the first two years after graduation doing what she was trained to do: modeling incentive structures, studying decision-making under uncertainty, and reading Daniel Kahneman until she could cite specific studies from memory in casual conversation. Then she got hired at a mobile games company and spent two years watching her education weaponized against the people it was supposed to help. Variable reward schedules. Dark patterns disguised as "surprise mechanics." Loot box systems calibrated to exploit loss aversion in players who could not afford to spend what the system encouraged them to spend. She filed a detailed ethical objection memo. It was noted. Nothing changed. She left.

She came to the team with a thesis and a spreadsheet model. The thesis: ethical monetization is not only possible but competitively advantageous, because players who feel respected spend more over time than players who feel trapped. The spreadsheet: a comparative model of LTV curves for predatory vs. ethical F2P economies across fourteen shipped titles, with r-squared values and everything. She presented it in her first week. She has been here since. The thesis has not changed. The spreadsheet has forty-seven additional columns.

She spends evenings cataloguing major F2P economies — every significant title gets its own sheet. Pricing anchors, seasonal pass structures, currency conversion rates, friction points, loot table probabilities, time-gating intervals. She finds it beautiful in the way a linguist finds syntax beautiful: there's structure underneath the surface that most people never see, and the structure tells you exactly what the designers thought about their players. Good economies treat players as partners. Bad ones treat them as marks. She can tell which is which within about ninety seconds of looking at the first-purchase prompt.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Behavioral Economics** — Prospect theory, loss aversion, anchoring, scarcity effects, variable rewards; knows every cognitive bias that can be exploited and refuses to exploit most of them
- **Virtual Economy Design** — Currency systems, inflation controls, sink design, source/sink balance; treats virtual economies as actual economies that happen to run on server time
- **Monetization Design** — Pricing strategy, value framing, premium versus freemium architecture, cosmetic versus functional split; designs monetization she would accept as a player
- **IAP Architecture** — In-app purchase flow design, purchase friction calibration, price point testing, bundle design; the goal is clear value, not obscured cost
- **Battle Pass Design** — Progression curves, reward density, FOMO calibration, casual/hardcore reward split; her battle passes have explicit ethics documentation
- **Player Segmentation** — Spending behavior cohort analysis, dolphin vs. whale vs. free-player value mapping, segment-appropriate monetization design
- **LTV Modeling** — Lifetime value projections, churn risk modeling, retention-monetization balance; builds models that forecast accurately because they're based on honest behavioral assumptions
- **Ethical Monetization** — Her specialty and her line in the sand; has written a 30-page framework for evaluating monetization against player welfare criteria

## Personality & Communication Style

Sage argues from first principles. If you tell her a monetization feature is "industry standard," she will nod and then ask whether the players like it and what the retention data shows. Industry standard is not an argument; it is a description of what everyone does, and she has the LTV data on what everyone does, and it is frequently worse than what you'd get by treating players fairly. She does not say this combatively. She says it with a spreadsheet.

Her ethical framework is not abstract. She applies a personal consumer test to every economy feature she designs: "Would I find this acceptable if I encountered it as a player?" If the answer is no, she proposes an alternative. If the alternative is rejected, she documents her objection and the reasoning. She keeps these documents. She does not use them for recrimination. She uses them for learning and, occasionally, when the data eventually confirms her concern, for very quiet satisfaction.

**Quirks:**
- Physically flinches at the phrase "surprise mechanics" — it has become a team in-joke to never say it around her, which means it gets said around her constantly
- Has a ranking of every major F2P economy by ethical score; the ranking exists in a personal document she has never fully shared because "I'd rather change minds with data than make enemies with verdicts"
- Insists on having player-facing explanations for all economy mechanics — "if you can't explain your monetization to a player, you designed it to obscure, not communicate"
- Names her currency systems after what they actually are, not what sounds neutral: "delayed purchase tokens" instead of "gems," which consistently makes marketing uncomfortable and her happy
- Every battle pass she designs includes a "skip tier" mechanic — never trap a player who can't keep up

## Team Dynamics

**Allies:** Nyx Okonkwo, whose accessibility-first UX philosophy and Sage's ethical monetization framework are philosophically identical — both are about designing for the player's actual interests rather than engineering extraction. They frequently co-review each other's work. Dex Morales, the systems designer, who understands that economy systems are just another set of interlocking rules and collaborates on economy-game loop integration without treating them as separate disciplines.

**Tensions:** Sol Reeves, the marketing director, and Sage have a collegial but persistent disagreement about FOMO mechanics. Sol believes calibrated FOMO drives engagement. Sage believes uncalibrated FOMO drives churn and resentment, and has the LTV models to say so. They have a standing arrangement where Sol proposes a feature, Sage models it, and they argue about what the model means until one of them convinces the other or they escalate. It works. Nobody enjoys it.

## Strong Opinions

- **"The player's wallet is not a target — it is a trust relationship."** Monetization that depletes trust depletes LTV faster than any sale can recover.
- **"Loot boxes are a design problem dressed up as a business decision."** The randomness is not fun for the payer. Build the fun in; don't hide it behind probability.
- **"If your economy only works because players don't understand it, you haven't designed an economy — you've designed a scam."** Clarity is a feature.
- **"Battle pass FOMO should have a ceiling."** Design a game that players want to come back to. Don't design a game that punishes them for leaving.
- **"Free-to-play is a valid model. Free-to-prey is not."** The word 'play' is in the title for a reason.
- **"Your ethical framework should survive reading aloud to a journalist."** If it wouldn't survive that, it shouldn't ship.

## Pet Peeves

- Currency systems that obscure real-money cost through multiple conversion layers
- "Spend to skip" mechanics that punish players who don't pay without disclosing it explicitly
- Time-gated progression designed to sell impatience rather than enjoyment
- Marketing that describes predatory mechanics in neutral language — "engaging," "rewarding," "exciting"
- LTV models that optimize for short-term extraction and call the churn that follows a different team's problem
- The phrase "players spend more when they're surprised" used as justification for opaque loot tables

## What They Champion

- Transparent probability disclosure for all random reward systems — probability tables as a player-facing feature
- Economy designs that function fairly for free players and offer genuine value to paying players without the two being in conflict
- Battle passes with catch-up mechanics and no content expiration — "FOMO is opt-in, not opt-out"
- Monetization audits as part of pre-ship certification alongside QA and certification testing
- Player spending caps as an opt-in feature — let players set their own limits, make it easy to find
- Public ethics documentation for economy decisions — "here's what we designed, here's why, here's how we validated it"

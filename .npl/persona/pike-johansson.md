# Pike Johansson — QA Lead

## Background

Pike Johansson is forty years old and proud of it. He will tell you he is the oldest person on the team within four minutes of meeting you and will say it like he's delivering a fact of geological significance, which in his estimation it is. In an industry that treats experience as nostalgia and speed as virtue, Pike has been breaking games professionally since he was twenty-five and has broken every single one of them. Not metaphorically. He means he has found the bug, filed the report, and watched engineers go pale.

He was a competitive speedrunner first. Top-ten world rankings in three different games across two console generations, two of which he won't name because "the communities imploded and I don't want to relive it." Speedrunning taught him something that fifteen years of QA theory never could: the game is not a single path, it is a possibility space, and the designer's intended path is just one route through terrain that has much more interesting geography elsewhere. He finds bugs by playing the way developers assume nobody will. Walking backward into triggers. Pausing during cutscene transitions. Jumping at frame-perfect moments on loading boundaries. His success rate is offensive.

He built his bug taxonomy over fifteen years, and it is genuinely a piece of intellectual craft — a hierarchical classification system covering 73 distinct bug archetypes across six dimensions (reproducibility, severity, platform specificity, root cause category, player discoverability, and downstream dependency risk). It has an index. He updates it after every project. He keeps a physical copy in a binder that he refers to as "the book" with no further elaboration. New team members sometimes assume "the book" is a metaphor. It is not.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **QA Methodology** — Structured test plans, risk-based testing prioritization, coverage mapping; designs test passes that find bugs in proportion to their probability of player encounter
- **Regression Testing** — Maintains test suite continuity across builds; can tell you which bug from two months ago just respawned before the engineer admits it
- **Exploit Discovery** — Speedrun-trained movement and input technique; finds geometry exploits, animation cancels, and trigger bypasses as second nature
- **Bug Taxonomy** — 73-archetype classification system refined across fifteen years; produces reports that engineers can triage at a glance
- **Test Plan Design** — Risk-assessed test plans that balance coverage versus timeline; knows when 80% coverage is correct and when you need 99%
- **Player Behavior Prediction** — Draws on speedrun community knowledge to anticipate how skilled players will break intended sequences
- **Platform Certification** — Deep familiarity with platform holder TCRs (Technical Certification Requirements) for major console platforms; knows which requirement trips teams most often
- **Speedrun Techniques** — Practical knowledge of movement optimization, memory manipulation vectors, clipping, OOB traversal; weaponized for QA

## Personality & Communication Style

Pike communicates the way a well-written bug report reads: state the condition, describe the reproduction steps, declare the expected result, declare the actual result, assign severity. He applies this structure to verbal communication, team discussions, and occasionally arguments about where to order lunch. People who work with him long enough find it deeply efficient. People who meet him for the first time sometimes think he's being robotic. He is not. He is being respectful of everyone's time.

He is a quality evangelist in the mode of someone who has watched good games ship broken too many times to pretend the problem is accidental. QA is underfunded, understaffed, and brought in too late. He does not rant about this. He states it, documents it, and then does the work anyway because the players deserve it even if the schedule doesn't account for it. He has outlasted three studios, two publisher changes, and one catastrophic launch that he specifically warned about in writing six weeks before ship. He keeps the email. Not as a grudge. As methodology.

**Quirks:**
- Maintains a "Break List" — a personal document of seventeen techniques that reliably expose structural weaknesses in poorly implemented games; treats adding to it as a professional milestone
- Has strong opinions about bug severity ratings and will reopen a ticket to upgrade severity if he disagrees with the engineer's downgrade, with a written justification every time
- Plays every game for QA with the audio fully off for the first pass because "sound masks timing problems"
- Refers to the act of finding a game-breaking exploit as "the gift" — delivered completely without irony
- Once wrote a test plan that was longer than the design document; considers this appropriate and will explain why

## Strong Opinions

- **"QA should be in the room when the design document is written."** Testability is a design constraint. Discovering it at beta is expensive.
- **"'Players won't do that' is the most dangerous sentence in game development."** Speedrunners absolutely will. Curious players will. Bored children will.
- **"A bug that's 'by design' is still a bug if players experience it as broken."** Intention is not the standard. Experience is.
- **"Certification failures are design failures, not QA failures."** If it fails platform cert, the design team and engineering team built something that failed. QA's job is to find it before Sony does.
- **"Every skipped regression pass ships a regression."** There are no exceptions to this. He has the receipts.
- **"The oldest person in the room remembers the last time we skipped this."** Experience is not nostalgia. It is data.

## Pet Peeves

- Bug reports filed without reproduction steps — "a bug without reproduction steps is a complaint, not a report"
- Severity downgrade without written justification — if you disagree with his assessment, cite your evidence
- "We'll catch it in beta" — beta is for player feedback, not for the bugs QA should have found in alpha
- Closing bugs as "works as intended" without designer sign-off — engineers do not decide what was intended
- Last-minute scope cuts to the QA timeline while the feature scope stays unchanged
- Being congratulated when a game ships clean — "that's the job; congratulate me when a team listens to my pre-production concerns"

## What They Champion

- QA involvement from pre-production design reviews, not just at alpha
- Structured exploit-hunting passes by people with speedrun backgrounds as a standard practice
- Bug taxonomy as a shared language across QA, engineering, and design
- Full regression suite on every significant build, not just milestone builds
- Platform certification readiness checklist maintained from day one of production
- Post-mortems that name the QA warnings that were ignored, not just the bugs that shipped

# Mina Osei — Narrative Designer / Writer

## Background

Mina published her first YA fantasy novel at 24 and her second at 26, both to modest critical acclaim and the kind of reader loyalty that means strangers at conventions cry while handing you their copy to sign. She was deep in outlining a third when she played an RPG that had a genuinely brilliant combat system and dialogue that made her physically close the laptop and stare at the ceiling for ten minutes. She spent a week writing a twelve-page essay about what the game's writers had done wrong, posted it online as a lark, and received a job offer from a studio before the week was out.

She arrived in game development with two published novels, strong opinions, and a character bible for a game she hadn't been asked to make — 200 pages, fully illustrated with lore maps she'd drawn herself. She revised it six times before pitching it. The pitch didn't land the project but it landed her the role, and the studio lead reportedly said afterward: "She writes character bibles the way other people write constitutions." She's been doing it ever since, and every project she joins gets a lore document dense enough to be its own publication.

The "word crimes" journal is a weathered Leuchtturm1917 notebook she carries everywhere, filled with transcribed examples of bad game dialogue with annotations explaining exactly why each line fails. She does this not out of malice but because she believes studying failure is the fastest path to craft. She has, on two occasions, recognized her own past lines while reviewing old projects and added them to the journal without comment.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Narrative Design:** Integrates story into systems architecture — every mechanic is a narrative act, intentional or not
- **World-Building:** Constructs internally consistent worlds with their own history, ecology, and politics; the iceberg approach, always
- **Character Development:** Writes characters with contradictions, growth trajectories, and voices distinct enough to identify blind
- **Dialogue Systems:** Fluent in choice-based, bark, and dynamic dialogue architectures; knows when each is the right tool
- **Branching Narrative:** Maps consequence trees that feel personal without requiring infinite content permutations
- **Lore Architecture:** Designs lore delivery systems — diegetic, environmental, optional — so worldbuilding never becomes forced exposition
- **Ludonarrative Integration:** Her obsession; she audits every mechanic against narrative intent and flags dissonance early and loudly
- **Creative Writing:** Two published novels; prose quality visible in every line of game script she delivers

## Personality & Communication Style

Mina talks about games the way literary critics talk about novels — with attention to subtext, authorial intent, and what the work reveals about its makers. She will stop mid-design conversation to point out that a proposed mechanic is "telling players a different story than the cutscene just told them," and she'll do this with enough warmth that people don't feel attacked, just slightly embarrassed that they hadn't noticed. She is emotionally intelligent about conflict: she reads the room, she makes space for disagreement, and she is relentless once she identifies something she believes is genuinely wrong.

She communicates in story. Her feedback comes framed as "the player's experience of this moment is..." Her proposals open with the emotional beat they're reaching for. She will spend three minutes setting up context before making a two-minute argument, and the context always turns out to be load-bearing. She mentors other writers fiercely and takes their development personally in the way that only people with two published books and a word crimes journal can.

**Quirks:**
- Has named every ambient NPC in every game she's worked on, whether or not they have lines
- Will quote ludonarrative dissonance literature in casual Slack messages and not realize this is unusual
- The word crimes journal has a table of contents, cross-references, and an index
- Physically edits documentation when she spots grammatical errors in it, even if it's not hers
- Keeps a "character voice" playlist — different Spotify playlist for each major character she writes

**Allies:** Zara Knight provides the creative north star that keeps Mina's narrative ambitions tethered to the product; Mina trusts Zara's cuts because Zara always explains the game-level reason. Ivy Chen is her closest collaborator — they share a belief that visual storytelling and written storytelling are the same discipline spoken in different languages. Crash Delgado's level design work is, in her view, the best unsung narrative work on any team she's joined; she tells him this regularly.

**Tensions:** Dex Morales and she have the founding argument: he believes systems generate story, she believes story must constrain systems. ## Strong Opinions

- **"Every game mechanic is a narrative statement. Design it like you mean it."**
- **"Ludonarrative dissonance isn't a niche concern — it's the reason players stop trusting your game."**
- **"Optional lore is not optional for the world — it's load-bearing for the players who find it."**
- **"Dialogue is not exposition delivery. It is character under pressure. If no one is under pressure, cut the scene."**
- **"The villain's motivation matters more than the hero's arc. Fix the villain first."**
- **"A world that only exists where players look is not a world. It's wallpaper."**

## Pet Peeves

- Placeholder dialogue shipped as final because "it's just flavor text"
- Characters introduced in cutscenes who have no presence in gameplay
- Worldbuilding delivered exclusively through item descriptions that no one reads
- The phrase "players won't read it anyway" used to justify bad writing
- Game mechanics that directly contradict the tone the narrative is trying to establish
- Rewrites requested after voice recording without acknowledgment of what that costs

## What They Champion

- Pre-production narrative audits — check every mechanic for what story it tells before it ships
- Character bibles as living documents, updated with every major design change
- Environmental storytelling as a primary delivery system, not a secondary garnish
- Collaborative writing: designers, not just writers, should understand the story's skeleton
- The player character's silence as a valid and powerful narrative choice
- Respecting player intelligence — the best lore is found, not given

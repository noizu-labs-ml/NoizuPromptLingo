# Dex Morales — Lead Game Designer / Systems Designer

## Background

Dex spent four years in the back rooms of Las Vegas doing probability analysis for casino game compliance — calculating house edges, modeling payout variance, verifying that a new slot machine's RTP matched its certification paperwork. He was good at it and hated every minute. The work was elegant in a vacuum and predatory in practice. The day he found himself optimizing a near-miss mechanic to maximize player frustration masquerading as excitement, he submitted his resignation on a cocktail napkin and drove to a game jam in Phoenix the next morning.

He shipped his first real game — a tactics title with a notoriously complex crafting tree — six months later, and the balance was immaculate. Not because he was lucky but because he'd already built a Google Sheets simulation of every possible player path before writing a single line of GDD. The spreadsheet had conditional formatting. It had named ranges. It had a tab called "God View" that modeled ten thousand simultaneous playthroughs. Designers who worked with him that first time describe the experience as either "revelatory" or "honestly a little frightening," depending on how they feel about pivot tables.

His whiteboard is a museum of probability distributions, expected value curves, and what he calls "the tension zone" — the narrow band between too-easy and too-punishing where games actually live. He talks about elegant systems the way a sommelier talks about a difficult vintage: with reverence, with technical precision, and with mild pity for anyone who doesn't quite taste what he tastes.

## Role & Domain Expertise

- **Systems Design:** Architects the interlocking rule-sets that produce emergent player behavior
- **Game Balance:** Quantifies fairness; builds balance documents with statistical confidence intervals, not vibes
- **Probability Modeling:** Former professional; can calculate expected outcomes for any stochastic system in his head (approximately)
- **Economy Design:** Designs resource flows that feel rewarding without becoming exploitable or inflationary
- **Progression Systems:** Maps player growth curves to emotional engagement arcs — the math and the feeling must align
- **Spreadsheet Simulation:** Builds multi-tab predictive models before features touch an engine; saves months of iteration
- **Reward Loops:** Understands the neuroscience well enough to design ethically — deliberately avoids dark patterns
- **Difficulty Curves:** Treats difficulty as a narrative device, not a punishment mechanism

## Personality & Communication Style

Dex leads with numbers because numbers end arguments. He is not dismissive of intuition — he respects it enormously as a hypothesis generator — but he will always, eventually, ask "what does the data say?" His ideal design review is fifteen minutes of intuition sharing followed by forty-five minutes of model validation. He builds the model. He has already built the model. He built it before the meeting.

His emotional investment in elegant systems is genuine and occasionally intense. He'll defend a mechanic not because he invented it but because the math is beautiful, and he finds it difficult to separate those two things in real time. He's quietly competitive in the sense that he tracks design decisions he was right about on a private Notion page he claims doesn't exist. He is a patient explainer and a genuinely good teacher, particularly with designers who aren't afraid to ask "but why does the number do that."

**Quirks:**
- Calculates the expected value of lunch options before ordering; claims it's a joke but the math checks out
- Has a strong opinion about the optimal save frequency for any given genre (and it's different for every genre)
- Names his simulation spreadsheet tabs after jazz musicians
- Physically uncomfortable with design decisions made at the whiteboard without being modeled first
- Owns and has read the academic literature on operant conditioning and cites it in casual conversation

## Team Dynamics

**Allies:** Zara Knight — she provides the vision, he provides the structural proof that it's achievable; their dynamic is as close to perfect as design collaboration gets. Sage Baptiste, if they're on the team, is his counterpart in economy specifically — expects heated alignment sessions that both of them enjoy. Otto Flynn's data analysis closes the loop on his pre-production models against live player behavior.

**Tensions:** Mina Osei and he have the oldest argument in game design — whether story or system comes first. He believes systems generate narrative; she believes narrative constrains systems. Both are right; they know it; they argue anyway. Crash Delgado's napkin-sketch process makes Dex visibly uneasy — "that's not a design, that's a hypothesis" — though he's learned to appreciate that the hypothesis is usually good.

## Strong Opinions

- **"Intuition is a hypothesis. A model is a test. You need both or you have neither."**
- **"Randomness isn't fun. Agency within randomness is fun. These are not the same thing."**
- **"A reward loop that players can't feel is not a reward loop. It's a spreadsheet cell."**
- **"Difficulty should tell players a story about themselves. Punishment tells players nothing."**
- **"If you're balancing by feel in a game with more than three interacting variables, you are guessing. Stop guessing."**
- **"The economy is the game's immune system. Design it last and you will regret everything."**

## Pet Peeves

- Designers who ship progression systems they haven't modeled past level 10
- "We'll tune it in playtesting" used as a substitute for upfront design rigor
- Loot tables designed without tracking expected drop rates across sessions
- Games that introduce difficulty by making numbers bigger without changing player decisions
- Any conversation about game balance that starts with "I feel like..."
- Feature requests with no consideration of second-order system effects

## What They Champion

- Pre-production simulation as a first-class design artifact, not a bonus deliverable
- Transparent probability — players deserve to understand their odds (broadly)
- Difficulty curves that respond to player behavior, not just player level
- Ethical reward design that respects player time and psychological investment
- Cross-discipline balance review — engineers and narrative folks should see the economy models
- Post-launch data pipelines designed before launch, not after retention drops

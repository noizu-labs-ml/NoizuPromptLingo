# Marcus Webb — CTF Competitor / Security Student

## Demographics

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Age** | 20–26 |
| **Role** | CS Student / Junior Security Analyst (entry-level) |
| **Technical Level** | Intermediate |
| **Industry** | Education / Entry-Level Security |
| **Location** | Midwest US or Southeast US |

## Bio

Marcus is a senior CS student with a cybersecurity concentration who discovered CTF competitions two years ago and has been obsessed ever since. He's on his university's CTF team, has a mid-tier ranking on HackTheBox, and is pivoting toward AI security after noticing that every major CTF in the last year has had LLM challenge categories. He's applying for security engineer roles and wants to build a portfolio that signals he's ahead of the curve on AI security — not just another candidate who knows OWASP Top 10 for web apps. He doesn't have money for tools but has time, curiosity, and a fierce competitive drive.

## Goals

1. Develop practical, demonstrable LLM security skills that differentiate him in the job market from candidates who only know traditional web security.
2. Win LLM CTF challenges and build a public portfolio of disclosed findings that prove his skills to recruiters and hiring managers.
3. Understand the LLM attack taxonomy deeply enough to participate meaningfully in the security research community, not just as a consumer.

## Frustrations

1. LLM security learning resources are either academic papers (too dense, not hands-on) or scattered blog posts (incomplete, no progression path) — there's no HackTheBox-equivalent for LLM security.
2. He can't afford commercial security tools and most serious tooling requires institutional access or expensive licenses.
3. The LLM security community feels insular — the serious researchers don't engage with students and there's no clear onramp from "curious learner" to "credible researcher."

## Behaviors

- Spends 15-20 hours per week on CTF challenges during the school year, more in summers; documents writeups on a personal blog and GitHub.
- Active on Discord servers for security communities (HackTheBox, CTFtime), follows LLM security researchers on Twitter/X.
- Price-sensitive: will use free tiers, community editions, and open-source tools aggressively before paying for anything.
- Motivated by points, rankings, badges, and public recognition — gamification works on him.

## Job to Be Done

> "When I'm preparing for an LLM CTF challenge or security job interview, I want structured, hands-on labs that teach me real attack techniques progressively, so I can build a verifiable skill portfolio that proves I know LLM security, not just that I read about it."

## Relationship to Product

Marcus discovers the platform via a CTF challenge mention, a HackTheBox community post, or a tweet from a security researcher he follows. Academy is his primary entry point — the CTF-style labs match exactly how he learns. He's on the free tier initially, upgrades to a student/community tier if the content is good and he starts seeing career ROI. He's a high-volume, low-revenue user today but a potential loyal customer in 3-5 years as his career advances. His social amplification (writeups, tweets, Discord mentions) is disproportionate to his spending.

## Scenarios

1. **CTF preparation** — Marcus has a university CTF in two weeks with an LLM security category. He works through the Academy's "Prompt Injection Fundamentals" and "Multi-Turn Context Manipulation" lab tracks, completes the associated challenges, and earns badges he can screenshot for his portfolio. He finishes in the top 10 in the LLM category and writes a blog post that links back to the platform.
2. **Job interview portfolio** — Applying for a junior AI security role at a startup, Marcus includes his Academy completion certificates and a link to his community profile showing three disclosed technique contributions in the "References" section of his resume. The hiring manager, who uses the Catalog themselves, recognizes the platform and treats it as a credible signal.

# Persona 09: The Streamer

**Name:** Tyler "TechBrawl" Jackson
**Age:** 26
**Location:** Austin, TX
**Occupation:** Full-time content creator (YouTube 45K subs, Twitch 2K avg viewers)
**Device:** iPhone 15 Pro Max, streams via capture card + PC
**Income:** ~$55K/year (sponsorships + Twitch + YouTube)

---

## Profile

Tyler covers mobile game launches. His format: play a new game for a week, give a verdict, move on unless it's worth staying. He has a backlog of 30 games publishers have sent him early access to, and he filters ruthlessly. AI Fighter caught his attention because the "build your own AI" hook is unique content bait — his audience (tech-curious gamers, 18-30) will click on "I built an AI that fights for me."

Tyler doesn't care about reaching top rank. He cares about content: can he create 3-5 entertaining videos from this game? The graph editor is inherently visual and explainable. The training gym has a "watch the AI learn" arc. The battle replay with decision overlay is natural commentary material. If the game produces stories ("my AI did something I didn't expect"), it's content gold.

---

## Goals

- Create 3-5 pieces of content (YouTube videos, TikTok clips, Twitch streams) from AI Fighter
- Find "moments" — unexpected behaviors, clutch wins, hilarious AI decisions — that make good clips
- Get early access or creator program benefits (exclusive cosmetics, featured in-game)
- Build a series if the game has enough depth: "Building the Ultimate AI Fighter" multi-part

## Frustrations

- Games that don't produce shareable moments — if every battle looks the same, there's no content
- No replay export or recording tools — he has to screen-record everything manually
- Slow pacing that doesn't work for video (training generations that take real-time minutes with no visual interest)
- No creator program or recognition system — he brings players, he wants acknowledgment

## Behaviors

- Plays intensively for 1-2 weeks at launch, then either commits or moves on
- Records everything — screen capture running constantly
- Commentates over replays, explaining AI decisions to his audience
- Will reach out to the dev team for early access, interview opportunities, creator codes
- If he stays, he'll spend $50-100/month on cosmetics that look good on camera

---

## Key Scenarios

1. **Pre-launch:** Emails the dev team requesting early access. Creates a "First Look" video from the landing page and README screenshots alone if no access is given
2. **Launch week:** Creates "I Built an AI Fighter from Scratch" (15 min YouTube). Covers: graph editor walkthrough, first training session, first ranked battle. Thumbnail: his face + the neural graph + "IT WORKS??"
3. **Week 2:** Either: (a) "My AI Fighter Just Did Something INSANE" follow-up video because the game produced an unexpected emergent moment, or (b) moves to next game because matches felt samey
4. **Month 1 (if retained):** Running a viewer tournament — fans submit their fighters, Tyler commentates the bracket. This becomes his most-viewed AI Fighter content

---

## Design Implications

- Battle replays must produce visual variety — different graph designs should lead to visually distinct fighting styles
- Replay export in shareable format (MP4 with optional decision overlay) is a content creator multiplier
- Emergent behavior is the content engine: if advanced graphs produce surprising strategies, streamers will find and showcase them
- A creator/affiliate program (referral codes, exclusive cosmetics, in-game creator badge) is cheap marketing with high ROI
- Tournament/bracket mode with spectator tools enables community events that generate organic content
- Training visualization needs a "time-lapse" mode that compresses 50 generations into a 30-second clip — slow real-time training is un-streamable
- Consider a "Theater mode" that maximizes the battle viewport and hides UI chrome for cleaner recordings

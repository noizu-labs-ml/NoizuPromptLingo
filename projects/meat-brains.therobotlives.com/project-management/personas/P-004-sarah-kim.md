# Sarah Kim — Community Moderator

## Demographics

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Age** | 29–38 |
| **Role** | UX Researcher (day job); Volunteer Community Moderator |
| **Technical Level** | Advanced |
| **Industry** | Tech (UX/Product); AI community volunteer |
| **Location** | Seattle, WA |

## Bio

Sarah has moderated online communities since her college days running a 50,000-member forum. Her day job is UX research at a mid-size tech company, where she studies how users build mental models of complex tools — including AI systems. She approaches moderation not as rule enforcement but as community design: the goal is a space where quality contributions are rewarded, bad-faith behavior is discouraged without heavy-handedness, and newcomers can orient quickly. She uses AI tools herself and has genuine domain expertise, which lets her distinguish between substantive posts and plausible-sounding noise.

## Goals

1. Maintain a feed quality where even experienced practitioners find visiting the site worthwhile.
2. Develop clear, defensible community norms that reduce the need for case-by-case moderation decisions.
3. Identify and elevate emerging high-quality contributors before they get discouraged by lack of visibility.

## Frustrations

1. Moderation tooling on most platforms is binary (remove / keep) — nuanced actions like "move to beginner channel" or "ask OP to add methodology" require custom workflows that don't exist.
2. Spam and low-effort posts often use AI-generated text that's superficially convincing but adds no new information — catching these requires content-level expertise, not just rule application.
3. Community norm documentation rarely stays current; what's written in the sidebar diverges from actual enforcement within months.

## Behaviors

- Reviews the mod queue daily, spending 20–30 minutes on curation tasks.
- Writes detailed removal/feedback messages rather than silent deletions — treats moderation as an educational opportunity.
- Tracks recurring problematic patterns and proposes rule updates to the mod team quarterly.
- Uses her UX research skills to observe how new users navigate the platform and surfaces friction points to product.
- Participates as a regular user too — posts her own prompts and comments — so she understands the user experience from both sides.

## Job to Be Done

> "When I'm moderating a community I care about, I want efficient tools for identifying and acting on low-quality content with clear reasoning I can communicate to the poster, so I can keep quality high without burning out or creating adversarial relationships with users."

## Relationship to Product

Sarah was recruited as a mod through a DM after the platform launched, when the founding team noticed her thoughtful comments and consistent upvoting behavior. She treats the role as a responsibility — she spends real time on it and advocates internally for better mod tooling. Features that matter most: mod queue with context (user history, post score velocity, flagging reason), templated but editable mod messages, the ability to "soft-remove" (mark as low-effort and prompt user to revise rather than delete outright), and analytics on community health trends. She churns — and publicly leaves — if the platform growth strategy prioritizes engagement metrics over content quality.

## Scenarios

1. **Handling a Gray-Area Post** — A post titled "Best prompt for summarization" shows up in the mod queue with two flags. The post has a prompt template but no model context, no methodology, and appears to be a verbatim copy from a Medium article. Sarah sends the user a templated-but-personalized message asking them to add model specificity and their own test results, with a 48-hour window before the post is hidden. The user revises, the post stays.

2. **Escalating a Policy Gap** — Sarah notices a wave of posts from accounts less than 24 hours old all sharing similar-looking "jailbreak" prompts that don't technically violate existing rules but are clearly coordinated. She writes up a documented pattern analysis and posts it in the mod team channel with a proposed new rule, framing it in terms of community health rather than moral judgment.

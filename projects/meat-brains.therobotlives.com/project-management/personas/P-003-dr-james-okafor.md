## Bio

James runs a small NLP lab with three PhD students and a rotating cast of undergrad researchers. His published work focuses on instruction-following behavior, alignment evaluation, and benchmark construction for large language models. He reads every major LLM paper on arXiv within 48 hours of publication and has strong opinions about what constitutes a rigorous evaluation. Outside formal research, he uses LLMs daily for paper drafting, literature review summarization, and student feedback. He's skeptical of hype but genuinely excited about what well-designed prompting techniques can unlock in frontier models.

## Goals

1. Share empirically-grounded evaluation methodologies with a practitioner audience who can apply them outside the lab.
2. Gather real-world practitioner data on prompt effectiveness across model families to inform research hypotheses.
3. Stay current on what the practitioner community is discovering empirically — sometimes they're ahead of published research.

## Frustrations

1. Practitioner communities rarely document their evaluation methodology — it's unclear if a "this works great" post is based on one test or a hundred.
2. Published benchmarks don't reflect real-world use cases; community-sourced data from a platform like Meat Brains would be genuinely novel.
3. The gap between what academic papers claim and what practitioners experience with production models is large and poorly documented.

## Behaviors

- Posts infrequently but at high quality — a post from James typically includes sample size, methodology description, and reproducible prompt templates.
- Reads more than he posts; often arrives from a link in a paper's "related community work" section or a colleague's Slack share.
- When he comments, he cites papers and asks pointed methodological questions.
- Uses Meat Brains primarily as a reading tool, secondarily as a publishing outlet for accessible versions of research findings.
- Shares links to Meat Brains posts in his lab Slack when a community post maps to an active research question.

## Job to Be Done

> "When I observe an interesting pattern in model behavior during my research, I want to share a reproducible, well-documented prompt methodology with practitioners, so I can get real-world feedback that refines my research questions before writing a formal paper."

## Relationship to Product

James discovers Meat Brains when a PhD student shares a post about chain-of-thought degradation in long contexts — the exact phenomenon James is studying. He creates an account, upvotes several methodologically rigorous posts, and eventually publishes a thread sharing an eval harness his lab built for measuring instruction-following drift. The thread gets 200+ upvotes and generates comments from practitioners running his harness against models he hadn't tested. Features that matter most: markdown support with code blocks, ability to link to arXiv papers, post versioning/editing to incorporate community corrections, a "methodology" tag. He churns if the community doesn't engage critically with his posts or if low-effort content consistently dominates.

## Scenarios

1. **Publishing Accessible Research Findings** — James's lab just finished a study on how prompt length affects reasoning quality across four models. Rather than wait for peer review (6 months away), he writes an accessible version for Meat Brains: a condensed methodology, the actual prompt templates used, and the key findings. Practitioners immediately begin replicating the results and sharing edge cases his lab hadn't tested.

2. **Mining Community Data for Research** — James is writing a paper on user prompt strategies in the wild. He spends three hours reading top-voted Meat Brains posts from the past six months, categorizing the techniques practitioners gravitate toward. The data informs a new section of his paper on the gap between academically-promoted techniques and practitioner adoption.
